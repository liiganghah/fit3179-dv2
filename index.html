Nineteen in a hundred

In 2021–22, nineteen in every hundred Australian children sang or played an instrument outside school. Four years earlier it was twenty-three. The children who still play usually take lessons, and they do not practise for long. The instruments crossing the border tell a second story: the piano used to lead the import bill, and the electric guitar has taken its place.

liiganghah · 14 October 2026. Written for a general reader in Australia. Participation from the Australian Bureau of Statistics, 2017–18 and 2021–22. Shipments from UN Comtrade, 2015–2024.

01

The children who still play

The Bureau of Statistics asks whether a child sang or played a musical instrument outside school hours in the previous year. Choir and piano are one answer. Informal singing around the house is not counted. In 2021–22 that answer was yes for 19.3 percent of children aged 5 to 14: about 621,000 children.

Figure 1. Isotype. Nineteen squares stand for the children who sang or played. The survey figure is 19.3 percent, so the last square is left empty rather than partly filled. Vega-Lite JSON

Figure 2. Slope chart. Each line is one creative activity. The heavy line is singing or playing. Click a name in the legend to follow that line alone. Vega-Lite JSON

Music was not alone. Drama, dancing and creative writing also fell. Art and craft did not move: 38.8 percent in both surveys. Reading for pleasure, which is not on this chart, fell from 78.5 to 72.4 percent. Screen-based activities stayed near 90 percent.

A limit of the survey. “Singing or playing” is a single question, so this page cannot say how many children play the guitar rather than sing in a choir. The shipping figures later on are instruments only. They answer a different question: not who plays, but what Australia buys.

02

A peak in the middle of primary school

Playing is lowest at ages 5 to 8 (16.1 percent), highest at 9 to 11 (23.8 percent), then 19.0 percent at 12 to 14. The other creative activities do not share that shape. Craft and dancing decline as children get older. Making digital content does the opposite: 8.4 percent of the youngest children, and about 20 percent from age 9. Music rises after the first years of school and is still common at the start of high school.

Figure 3. Heatmap. The number in each cell is the percent of children in that age group. Art and craft is the darkest row because it is the most common, not because it is the subject. The shape of the music row is the point. Vega-Lite JSON

03

After school, almost everyone stops

The adult question is stricter. It counts singing or playing as a soloist or in a group, including lessons and practice, and it leaves out karaoke and singing around the house. On that definition only 3.8 percent of people aged 15 and over took part in 2021–22: about 770,000 people. The highest adult age group is 15 to 24, at 6.7 percent. From the late twenties the rate stays near 3 percent. Men (4.4 percent) are slightly ahead of women (3.2 percent).

Figure 4. Line chart. Hover a point for the margin of error. The Bureau’s 95 percent margin is the range the result would usually fall in if the survey were repeated. Vega-Lite JSON

Do not read the child charts and this chart as one continuous line. A 14-year-old and a 16-year-old were asked different questions. The fall from about one child in five to about one adult in twenty-six is still the point: playing is common at school age and uncommon afterwards.

04

Lessons, then a short practice

Among children who sing or play, about two in three take lessons (401,700 of 620,700). Inside that group, the largest block is children who kept going for more than 13 weeks and still did under two hours in a usual week: 58.7 percent of the lesson-takers. Children with no lessons have the same habit. More than half of them also stayed with it for most of the year and practised very little.

Figure 5. Marimekko. The printed percentages are the share inside that column, not of all children. A few thousand children are missing between the player total and the lesson split because the Bureau rounds each cell. Vega-Lite JSON

05

Hearing an instrument, state by state

The Bureau does not publish singing or playing by state. It does publish whether children went to a live music performance outside school. That is not the same as learning an instrument. It is the public side of the same musical life: a concert, a gig, a recital.

Nationally, 12.3 percent of children aged 5–14 had been to live music in the year. New South Wales (8.7 ± 1.8) and Victoria (9.2 ± 1.7) sit below Queensland (16.0 ± 3.8) and Western Australia (18.4 ± 4.1). Those ranges do not overlap. The Northern Territory is the darkest colour, at 23.4, but its margin is ±6 points, so it is not safe to call it first.

Figure 6. Choropleth, the first of three maps. The projection is conic equal-area, so a rate can be compared across states without stretching Western Australia. Hover for the published percent and the margin of error. Vega-Lite JSON

06

The instruments Australia actually buys

Customs records are the public, instrument-by-instrument account of what entered the country. They are not a count of students. A shop, a school and a living-room buyer are mixed together, and an instrument made in Australia never appears. The fair comparison is the money, in US dollars, because a pair of castanets and a drum kit can both be “one item”.

$112m import bill in 2015. Acoustic pianos were the largest family.
$200m the peak, in 2022. Electric guitars alone were $58 million.
$141m the 2024 bill. Electric guitars were still first, at $40 million.

Figure 7. Bump chart. “Electric guitars” is the customs heading for electric instruments that are not keyboards, so a small number of other electric instruments sit in that line. “Other wind” includes recorders, flutes, clarinets and harmonicas. Acoustic pianos here include uprights, grands, and a small residual keyboard heading. Vega-Lite JSON

Rank hides the size of the change. From 2015 to 2024 the bill rose by about $29 million, and electric guitars account for about $20 million of that. Acoustic guitars, as a bill, are slightly smaller than they were. Percussion did not move.

Figure 8. Waterfall. The black bars are the total bill in 2015 and 2024. The floating bars are the change for each family, in millions of US dollars. Vega-Lite JSON

The same 2024 bill, drawn as area. Electric guitars are the largest block, then electric keyboards, then acoustic pianos. Australia imported 1,115 grand pianos and 4,829 uprights. An electric guitar, of which about 134,000 arrived, is a much smaller object. That is why the count and the bill tell different stories, and why the picture uses the bill.

Figure 9. Treemap. Area is 2024 import value. The thin brass strip is left unlabelled so the type stays readable. The piano count in the hover is uprights plus grands (5,944). A residual keyboard heading of about $280,000 is included in the piano dollars and is not counted as pianos. Vega-Lite JSON

07

Where those instruments come from

In 2024, four origins accounted for most of the value: China ($37 million), the United States ($25 million), Japan ($22 million) and Indonesia ($18 million). Germany and Malaysia followed, at about $8 million each. The circles use area, not the size of the country. A choropleth of these dollars would have made China look important partly because China is large.

Figure 10. Proportional symbol map, the second map. Equal Earth projection. Circles under $1 million are omitted. The country is the origin recorded by customs, which is usually where the instrument was made or last shipped from, not the brand on the headstock. Hover for the largest instrument family on that route: Japan and Germany are piano routes, the United States is an electric-guitar route, Malaysia is keyboards. Vega-Lite JSON

Drawn again, the six large origins become lines into Australia. Width is the same value as the circles. Colour is the largest family on that route, which is where the specialisation shows: Japan and Germany are piano routes, the United States is an electric-guitar route, and Malaysia is keyboards. China’s line is coloured as electric guitars because that is its largest slice, about a quarter of the value. The rest is spread across other families.

Figure 11. Flow map, the third map. Equal Earth again. Only origins of $8 million or more are drawn, so the six lines stay readable. Vega-Lite JSON

08

What the two records say together

Playing an instrument, or singing in a counted setting, is a childhood activity with a clear age: the middle of primary school. It became a little less common between the two surveys, in step with most other creative activities, while craft held its ground. People who keep playing into adulthood are a small group.

When children do play, the usual pattern is a lesson and a short practice. The instruments the country now spends the most money importing are not the acoustic piano of 2015. They are electric guitars and electric keyboards, and they arrive from a short list of countries.

Domain, reader, purpose

Domain. Music education in Australia: who takes part, at what age, how much they practise, and which instruments are in circulation.

Who. A general reader in Australia. No statistical training is assumed. “Margin of error” is explained in ordinary language where it changes what a chart is allowed to claim.

Why. A parent or a student cannot see, from a shop or a school newsletter, whether children still learn instruments, or whether the piano is still the instrument the country invests in.

What. Two public sources, neither altered. Australian Bureau of Statistics, Cultural and creative activities, 2021–22, released 21 April 2023, including the 2017–18 comparison in that release. abs.gov.au. United Nations Comtrade, Australia’s reported imports of pianos, strings, wind, percussion and electric instruments, 2015 to 2024. Values are in US dollars. comtradeplus.un.org.

How the pictures were chosen

  1. Isotype. Turns 19.3 percent into a count that can be seen without a scale.
  2. Slope chart. Two survey years, six activities. The task is “what moved, and what held still?”
  3. Heatmap. Music’s age shape is not the same as craft, dance or digital making.
  4. Line chart. A rate across ordered adult ages. A basic idiom, used because that is the comparison.
  5. Marimekko. Column width is lessons or not. Height is the kind of year.
  6. Choropleth. A rate by state. Conic equal-area, so area stays comparable. Counts are not coloured, because big states would win for being big.
  7. Bump chart. Rank of each instrument family, 2015 to 2024. The crossing in 2019 is the event.
  8. Waterfall. Turns those rank changes back into dollars.
  9. Treemap. The 2024 bill as areas, so a family that ranks high on a small amount cannot look like the whole story.
  10. Proportional symbol map. Import value by origin. Equal Earth. Area of the circle encodes dollars.
  11. Flow map. The same origins as routes into Australia. Width is value. The lines are schematic, not shipping lanes.

Hover is used where a printed number would crowd the picture. Clicking a legend item is used only where one line among many is the thing a reader wants to trace. Nothing on the page swaps out a section.
