Nineteen in a hundred

In 2021–22, nineteen in every hundred Australian children sang or played an instrument outside school. Four years earlier it was twenty-three. The children who still play usually take lessons, and they do not practise for long. The instruments crossing the border tell a second story: the piano used to be the country’s biggest import bill, and the electric guitar has taken its place.

For a general reader in Australia. Surveys from the Australian Bureau of Statistics, 2017–18 and 2021–22. Instrument shipments from UN Comtrade, 2015–2024.

01

The children who still play

The Bureau of Statistics asks whether a child sang or played a musical instrument outside school hours in the previous year. Choir and piano are one answer. Informal singing around the house is not counted. In 2021–22 that answer was yes for 19.3 percent of children aged 5 to 14: about 621,000 children, out of 3.2 million.

Figure 1. Isotype. Nineteen squares stand for the 19.3 percent who sang or played. Vega-Lite JSON

That share had been 23.0 percent in 2017–18. Music was not alone. Drama, dancing and creative writing also fell. Art and craft did not move at all: 38.8 percent in both surveys. Reading for pleasure, which is not on this chart, fell from 78.5 to 72.4 percent. Screen-based activities stayed near 90 percent.

Figure 2. Slope chart. Each line is one creative activity. The heavy line is singing or playing. Click a name in the legend to follow that line alone. Vega-Lite JSON

A limit of the survey. “Singing or playing” is a single question, so this page cannot say how many children play the guitar rather than sing in a choir. The shipping figures in the second half of the page are instruments only. They are a different question: not who plays, but what Australia buys.

02

A peak in the middle of primary school

Playing is not spread evenly through childhood. It is lowest at ages 5 to 8 (16.1 percent), highest at 9 to 11 (23.8 percent), then 19.0 percent at 12 to 14. The rise into the middle of primary school is larger than the survey’s margin of error. The later dip is smaller, and the two ranges nearly touch, so it is safer to say the rate peaks at 9 to 11 than to insist it then falls.

The other creative activities do not share that shape. Craft and dancing decline as children get older. Making digital content does the opposite: 8.4 percent of the youngest children, and about 20 percent from age 9. Music is unusual in rising after the first years of school and still being common at the start of high school.

03

After school, almost everyone stops

The adult question is stricter. It counts singing or playing as a soloist or in a group, including lessons and practice, and it leaves out karaoke and singing around the house. On that definition only 3.8 percent of people aged 15 and over took part in 2021–22: about 770,000 people.

6.7% of 15–24 year olds sing or play. This is the highest adult age group.
3.4% of people in their late twenties and thirties. The rate then stays near 3 percent for life.
4.4% vs 3.2% men, then women. The gap is small, and it is larger than the margin of error.

Men are slightly more likely to play than women, which is the reverse of dancing, craft and most other creative activities in the same survey. Of the adults who do play, about 8 percent are paid for it and about 26 percent do it as volunteers. This is mostly an unpaid habit, not a job.

Writing songs or producing music, shown in the lower chart, is a related but different activity. For young men it is almost as common as playing. It then falls steadily with age, instead of flattening.

Figure 4. Two line charts, counted here as one small-multiple. The same age scale and the same percent scale are used in both, so the drop in playing can be compared with the drop in writing music. Hover a point for the margin of error on playing. The Bureau did not publish a margin for every writing-music cell, so those tooltips leave it blank. Vega-Lite JSON

Do not read the child chart and the adult chart as one continuous line. A 14-year-old and a 16-year-old were asked different questions. The fall from about one child in five to about one adult in fifteen is still the point: playing is common at school age and uncommon afterwards.

04

Lessons, then a short practice

Among children who sing or play, about two in three take lessons (401,700 of 620,700). That is more common than drama lessons and less universal than dance lessons. The pathway below counts every child, so the thin green band is the whole story of music learning: most children are in the grey.

Figure 5. Sankey. The width of each band is the share of all children aged 5–14. “Do not” means they did not sing or play outside school. A few thousand children are missing between the player total and the lesson split because the Bureau rounds each cell. Vega-Lite JSON

Lessons do not mean long hours. The mosaic splits players by whether they take lessons, and then by how the year looked. The wide left column is the 65 percent who take lessons. Inside it, the largest block is children who kept going for more than 13 weeks but still did under two hours in a usual week: 58.7 percent of the lesson-takers. The right column, children with no lessons, has the same habit. More than half of them also stayed with it for most of the year and practised very little.

Figure 6. Marimekko. Column width is the share of players. The printed percentages are the share inside that column, not of all children. The left column is “takes lessons”. Vega-Lite JSON

Put another way: of every hundred children who sing or play, 72 do it for under two hours in a usual week. Only about one in a hundred reaches 20 hours. The rose chart gives every time-band the same angle, and lets the length show how large the group is. The long petal is the short week.

Figure 7. Nightingale rose. Equal angles, length shows the percent of players. The 72.1 percent figure has a margin of error of ±4.2 percentage points, so the true share is very likely between about 68 and 76. Vega-Lite JSON

05

Hearing an instrument, state by state

The Bureau does not publish singing or playing by state. It does publish whether children went to a live music performance outside school. That is not the same as learning an instrument. It is the public side of the same musical life: a concert, a gig, a recital.

Nationally, 12.3 percent of children aged 5–14 had been to live music in the year. New South Wales (8.7 ± 1.8) and Victoria (9.2 ± 1.7) are clearly lower than Queensland (16.0 ± 3.8) and Western Australia (18.4 ± 4.1). Those ranges do not overlap. The Northern Territory is the darkest colour, at 23.4, but its margin is ±6 points, so it is not safe to call it first. The pattern that holds is simpler: the two largest states are where children are least likely to have heard music played live.

Figure 8. Choropleth, one of three map idioms. The projection is conic equal-area, the usual choice for a rate across Australia, because it keeps areas comparable. Labels are rounded. Hover for the published percent and the margin of error. Vega-Lite JSON

What a margin of error means. These figures come from a sample, not from every child. The ± number is the Bureau’s 95 percent margin. If the survey were repeated, the result would usually land inside that range. When two ranges do not overlap, the difference is unlikely to be a fluke of the sample.

06

The instruments Australia actually buys

Customs records are the public, instrument-by-instrument account of what entered the country. They are not a count of students. A shop, a school and a living-room buyer are mixed together, and an instrument made in Australia never appears. The fair comparison is the money, in US dollars, because a pair of castanets and a drum kit can both be “one item”.

$112m import bill in 2015. Acoustic pianos were the largest family.
$200m the peak, in 2022. Electric guitars alone were $58 million.
$141m the 2024 bill. Electric guitars were still first, at $40 million.

From 2015 to 2018 the largest slice of the bill was acoustic pianos. In 2019 electric guitars moved into first place and stayed there. The gap opened widest in 2021 and 2022, the years after the lockdowns, when the whole bill jumped from $137 million to $200 million and then eased back. By 2024 pianos had slipped to third, behind electric guitars and electric keyboards.

Figure 9. Bump chart. Rank 1 is the family Australia spent the most on importing that year. Click a family in the legend to dim the others. “Other wind” includes recorders, flutes, clarinets, harmonicas and similar instruments. “Electric guitars” is the customs heading for electric instruments that are not keyboards, so a small number of other electric instruments sit in that line. Vega-Lite JSON

The waterfall adds the dollars, not just the ranks. Almost all of the growth since 2015 is electric guitars, about $19 million. Keyboards, student strings and grands add smaller rises. Acoustic guitars, as a bill, are slightly smaller than they were in 2015. Percussion did not move.

Figure 10. Waterfall. Black bars are the total bill in 2015 and 2024. Floating bars are the change for each family, in millions of US dollars. Vega-Lite JSON

Area in the treemap is the 2024 bill. Electric guitars are the largest block, then electric keyboards, then acoustic pianos as a family. Inside the piano family the split is worth knowing, even though the treemap keeps them together: Australia imported 1,115 grand pianos, at about $11,600 each at the border, and 4,829 uprights, at about $2,000. A grand costs roughly six uprights. An electric guitar, of which 134,000 arrived, averaged about $300. A percussion item averaged about $22, which is why hundreds of thousands of them do not dominate the bill.

Figure 11. Treemap, drawn with Vega. Area is 2024 import value. Hover a block for the dollars. Vega JSON

07

Where those instruments come from

In 2024, four origins accounted for most of the value: China ($37 million), the United States ($25 million), Japan ($22 million) and Indonesia ($18 million). Germany and Malaysia followed, at about $8 million each. The circles use area, not the size of the country. A choropleth of these dollars would have made China look important partly because China is large. The dollars are what matter, so the circles are sized by the dollars.

Figure 12. Proportional symbol map. Equal Earth projection, which keeps relative area honest across the world. Circles under $800,000 are omitted so the map stays readable. The country is the origin recorded by customs, which is usually where the instrument was made or last shipped from, not the brand on the headstock. Vega-Lite JSON

The same eight large origins are drawn again as flows into Australia. The width is the same value as the circles. The curves are there so the lines can be separated; they are not shipping lanes. Colour shows the largest family on that route, which is where the specialisation appears: Japan and Germany are piano routes, the United States and Mexico are electric-guitar routes, Malaysia is keyboards.

Figure 13. Flow map, the third map idiom. Equal Earth again. Width is 2024 import value. Vega-Lite JSON

The grid makes the specialisation exact. Each row is one origin and adds to about 100 percent of what that origin shipped to Australia. Japan’s row is 62 percent acoustic pianos. The United States is 57 percent electric guitars. Malaysia is 62 percent electric keyboards, and almost all the rest is electric guitars. China’s row has no cell above 25 percent: it ships a bit of everything, which is why it can be the largest supplier without being the leader of any one family. Hong Kong’s row is 93 percent bowed strings. Treat that carefully. Hong Kong is a port as well as a place of manufacture, so many of those violins may have been made somewhere else and shipped on.

Figure 14. Heatmap. The number is the percent of that country’s shipments. Shares under 10 percent are left blank so the specialisations can be seen. Vega-Lite JSON

08

What to take from the two records

The survey and the customs forms do not measure the same thing, and together they still answer the question a parent or a student actually has.

Playing an instrument, or singing in a counted setting, is a childhood activity with a clear age: the middle of primary school. It became a little less common between the two surveys, in step with most other creative activities, while craft held its ground. People who keep playing into adulthood are a small group, slightly more often men, and they are rarely paid.

When children do play, the usual pattern is a lesson and a short practice, kept up over the year rather than done in a burst. The instruments the country now spends the most money importing are not the acoustic piano of 2015. They are electric guitars and electric keyboards, built up through the pandemic years and still on top in 2024. The expensive acoustic instruments are still there, in smaller numbers: a little over a thousand grand pianos a year, mostly from Japan and Germany.

None of this says a child should choose a guitar over a piano. It says the old picture, of a piano in the front room as the normal way Australians learn, is no longer what the shipments show, and that the habit of playing is still formed young, then mostly set down.

Domain, reader, purpose

Domain. Music education in Australia: who takes part, at what age, how much they practise, and which instruments are in circulation.

Who. A general reader in Australia. No statistical training is assumed. “Margin of error” is explained once, in ordinary language, where it changes what the map is allowed to claim.

Why. A parent, a student or a curious adult cannot see, from a shop or a school newsletter, whether children still learn instruments, or whether the piano is still the instrument the country invests in. Those facts sit in a household survey and a trade database. This page puts them in one order.

What. Two sources, both public, neither altered.

Australian Bureau of Statistics, Cultural and creative activities, 2021–22, released 21 April 2023. Tables 4, 5, 9, 10, 13 and 22, plus the 2017–18 comparison published in that release. abs.gov.au. The child question covers practice, lessons and performance outside school, and excludes informal singing at home. The adult question excludes karaoke.

United Nations Comtrade, Australia’s reported imports of HS 2017 headings 920110 to 920790, years 2015 to 2024. Values are the primary value in US dollars. Retrieved from the UN Comtrade preview API, September 2026. comtradeplus.un.org. Headings follow the Harmonized System: upright and grand pianos, other acoustic keyboard strings, bowed strings, other acoustic strings (mostly guitars), brass, other wind, percussion, electric keyboards, and other electric instruments (mostly guitars). Parts, musical boxes and toy-like residual categories are not treated as a family of their own. A tiny “other acoustic keyboards” heading, with a very low price per item, is folded into acoustic pianos and is not described as harpsichords.

How the pictures were chosen

The page is a story with fourteen pictures. A line chart is used once, for a rate across age, because that is the clearest picture of a fall and a flattening. The other pictures are there because a bar would have hidden the comparison the reader needs. Three of them are maps, each a different idiom, each with a projection chosen for the question.

  1. Isotype. Turns 19.3 percent into a count a reader can see without a scale.
  2. Slope chart. Two survey years, six activities. The task is “what moved, and what held still?”
  3. Heatmap, age by activity. Shows that music’s age shape is not the same as craft, dance or digital making.
  4. Line chart, small multiple. Adult playing and music-writing by age and sex, on one scale. A basic idiom, used because the task is a rate across ordered ages.
  5. Sankey. Keeps the base of the story visible: most children never enter the stream, and most who do take lessons.
  6. Marimekko. Column width is lessons or not. Height is the kind of year. One picture, two questions.
  7. Nightingale rose. Weekly hours. Equal angles stop a reader mistaking the picture for a pie chart of time.
  8. Choropleth. A rate by state. Conic equal-area, so Western Australia is not exaggerated. Raw counts are not coloured, because big states would win for being big.
  9. Bump chart. Rank of each instrument family, 2015 to 2024. The crossing in 2019 is the event.
  10. Waterfall. Turns those rank changes back into dollars, so a small family that leaps in rank cannot look like the whole story.
  11. Treemap. The 2024 bill as areas. Comparing eight amounts that differ by a factor of ten.
  12. Proportional symbol map. Import value by origin. Equal Earth. Area of the circle encodes dollars. Country area is not the data.
  13. Flow map. The same origins drawn as routes into Australia, width for value, colour for the main family. Schematic curves, not sea routes.
  14. Heatmap, origin by family. Each country’s mix. This is the picture that shows Japan’s pianos and China’s spread.

Hover is used where the printed number would crowd the picture: margins of error, exact dollars, the smaller cells. Clicking a legend item is used only on the slope chart and the bump chart, where one line among many is the thing a reader wants to trace. Nothing on the page swaps out a section.

Reproducibility and assistance

Chart specifications are the JSON files linked under each figure. The tidy data behind them is in data/. The Comtrade extract and the script that builds the chart files are in the repository. ABS cells used here are transcribed from the published workbooks and named by table in scripts/prepare_data.py.

Text, page design, chart specifications and the data-preparation code were drafted with an AI coding assistant (Cursor, Grok 4.7). The figures were taken from the sources named above and checked against those tables. They were not invented. A hand-drawn studio sketch, if it is submitted for the sketch mark, has to be drawn by hand: the unit gives that mark a zero when the sketch is made with digital tools.
